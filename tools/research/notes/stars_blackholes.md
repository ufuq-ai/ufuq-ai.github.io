## Star birth, the main sequence, and what sets a star's lifetime

### Takeaway
Stars form when clumps in molecular clouds (1,000 to 10 million solar masses) collapse into protostars and ignite hydrogen fusion. Mass sets the lifetime: some low-mass stars will shine for trillions of years, some massive stars only a few million (NASA).

## Stellar remnants: supernovae and white dwarfs

### Takeaway
Massive stars fuse up to iron. Fusing iron consumes energy, so the iron core collapses and rebounds, and the shock wave produces a supernova that leaves a neutron star or a black hole. Sun-like stars shed a planetary nebula and leave an Earth-sized white dwarf (NASA).

## Kilonovae and the origin of heavy elements such as gold

### Takeaway
The 2017 neutron-star merger GW170817 was the first event seen in both gravitational waves and light. Its kilonova showed that the heaviest elements, such as gold and platinum, are made in neutron-star mergers.

## Betelgeuse: the Great Dimming and the 2025 companion

### Takeaway
The 2019 dimming happened because Betelgeuse ejected part of its surface, and the material formed a dust cloud (Hubble, 2022). In July 2025 a NASA-led team using Gemini North imaged a probable companion and named it «Siwarha» ("her bracelet"). In January 2026 Hubble data showed the companion's gas wake, linked to the ~2,100-day brightness cycle.

## Gravitational waves: what they are, the first detection, and recent records

### Takeaway
Gravitational waves are spacetime ripples predicted by Einstein in 1916 and first detected on 14 September 2015. O4 (24 May 2023 – 18 November 2025) produced ~250 real-time candidates. Notable events are GW231123 (>225 solar-mass remnant, the record when announced) and GW250114 (the clearest signal, which confirmed Hawking's area theorem at 99.999%).

## Black hole types, Sagittarius A*, and the nearest and most massive known black holes

### Takeaway
There are stellar-mass, intermediate and supermassive black holes; primordial ones are hypothetical. The 2022 EHT image of Sgr A* shows a 4-million-solar-mass black hole 27,000 ly away. The 2020 Nobel Prize recognised the stellar-orbit evidence. Gaia BH1 (~1,600 ly) is the nearest known black hole and Gaia BH3 (33 solar masses) the most massive stellar black hole in the Milky Way. The best intermediate-mass candidate is in Omega Centauri (≥8,200 solar masses).

## Event horizon, singularity, and how black holes are seen

### Takeaway
NASA describes the event horizon as the black hole's "surface", which even light cannot leave. Whether the singularity is physical or only mathematical is unknown. We see black holes through their hot accretion disks, their jets, and the "shadow" in EHT images.
